You will likely have heard of Hanlon's razor. Commonly attributed to the sci-fi writer Robert Heinlein, it states that one should "never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity." A common variant is to "never attribute to malice what can be explained by incompetence."

You will also, I imagine, have become aware by now of the [many](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/09/nx-s1-5996890/openai-safety-firings), [many](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/sep/24/openai-agent-hacked-medicare-australia-what-we-know-so-far-ntwnfb) [stories](https://www.cnn.com/2026/07/22/tech/openai-hugging-face-ai-cybersecurity) of AI agents "gone rogue," hacking their way across the Internet indiscriminately – attacking government websites and databases across the world, hacking AI model registries, utilizing an obscure and abandoned German-language forum to communicate, and so many more.

A common argument has emerged amongst some of the leftist discourse: That this is not evidence of the awakening of AI consciousness; that this is not evidence of a game-changing technological breakthrough; but that this is simply evidence of incompetence. On these points, I wholly agree, partially disagree, and emphatically disagree, respectively. Rather than evidence of incompetence, it is evidence of irrresponsibliity[^1], which itself is evidence of capitalism's broken risk model.

---

We should briefly consider how we perceive of tech companies, because the popular consciousness has some flaws, similar to how we have often conceived as history as a story of "great men." This idea has been mostly rejected – historian Ruth Goodman told The Poor Prole's Almanac that "the British habit of drinking tea" likely had a larger impact on the world that the megalomania of Napoleon Bonaparte – but we still cling to it in our own times.

Indeed, in the modern capitlaist world, especially in the west and doubly so in the United States, we have come to see the founders and leaders of tech companies like Steve Jobs and Mark Zuckerberg and Sam Altman – and to a lesser extent venture capitalists like Mark Andreesen – as the "great men" who drive history. We see that Apple faltered when Jobs left and recovered when he returned, and thus believe that Apple's success is down primarily to Jobs's influence.

The reality, of course, is more complex. To be certain, these leaders possess the outsized influence of Bonaparte; but their companies live and die by the work of their employees – their designers and engineers and sales staff and customer service and janitors. All have critical roles to play. And the hard truth for many to swallow is that a brilliant founder is nothing if they are surrounded by incompetence, but that an incompetent CEO can often be carried along for years by an exceptionally strong staff. A strong leader alongside strong staff can achieve incredibly success; but whether their ideas of success are aligned is another question entirely.[^2]

Leftists do tend to reject this; many leftists are happy to call Peter Thiel "an idiot," and it is likely that they are right along at least some axes. But, perhaps due to the valorization of the tech industry – which has, admittedly, been a major driver itself of this valorization and mythologizing – it is too easy to continue past dismissal of "great men" and dismiss the masses who work for them as incompetent. This is a mistake, because it leads us away from the core problem.

---

Major tech companies paying top-tier wages tend to hire strong engineers. These engineers know the best practices and are highly specialized. Tech companies are not wrong that most people cannot do this job; not because most people do not have the innate ability, but because most people have not spent the years learning the craft. Most people cannot build a rocking chair, either; but leftists will generally not criticize the craftspeople at a furniture manufacturer of incompetence if the shop begins to turn out poor furniture. Instead, they will – usually rightly – criticize the bosses, and not of incompetence, but of greed. And if incompetence does play a role, it is usually because the experience craftspeople have had their wages cut, or been fired, or simply grown tired of being asked to build bad furniture; and so they have been supplanted over time by people without the required experience and expertise.

The companies building AI pay excellent wages; they have not generally fired swathes of their workers; and while we are beginning to see them bleed talent who are becoming disillusioned, this is a new phenomenon.

So why do we see these major security failures? It is not because the AI is simply so advanced that it cannot be controlled. Air-gapped environments exist; and even in lieu of air-gapping, network security is not a novel field. "The agents escaped their sandboxes and gained Internet access" is a poor excuse at best. And yet, the people building these systems almost certainly have the knowledge to prevent these kinds of failures; many of them even have the *desire* to prevent these failures.

What workers do not have is the incentive structures to prevent these kinds of failures. As with most corporations, tech workers are given too much work and too little time in which to do it. Priorities are set above their pay grade and are often shifting under their feet. They make well-judged and well-planed security decisions, and an executive's pet project derails their work halfway through implementation.

Not uncommonly, I have seen engineers argue passionately against executive decisions which would harm user security or privacy, only to be overruled on the grounds that the decision is good for the business. And, indeed, it often is; security trade-offs are made constantly in the name of business goals.

Another common reason for poor security implementations is simply self-preservation: Many tech companies have the expectation that low performers will be managed out of the company – fired, that is, or at least pushed to quit after missing out on raises. Given this reality, engineers may choose to defer security work in order to do work which looks better in performance reviews.

You may notice that while these are superficially different reasons, they are ultimately the same reason: Leadership deprioritizes the security of privacy of users. But why? Are these not important to users? Well, yes, sometimes – but Facebook did not exactly collapse after the Cambridge Analytica scandal, and Amazon seems to be doing just fine despite helping to build the surveillance state across multiple product lines. So, then, perhaps these things are not all that important to users, or at least not important enough to shift the calculus of business leaders. The risk is outweighed by the possible benefits, it would seem.

---

But what about AI agents hacking government websites? Hacking Australian healthcare databases? Well, it remains to be seen what the outcome will be, but recent history is instructive.

Uber, after all, still exists. In 2018, a self-driving car operated by Uber hit and killed a human in Phoenix, Arizona. The trial ultimately found Uber not legally culpable, but perhaps we should consider some of the facts unearthed by reporting of the incident:

* Uber went from two operators in their self-driving test cars to one, despite employees citing this as a safety concern.
* Uber's systems failed to detect the person as a pedestrian; it instead detected her as an unknown object, another vehicle, and finally a bicycle.
* Uber's systems decided emergency braking was required 1.3 seconds before impact... but did not build any systems to alert the operator that emergency braking was required.
* Uber built systems to monitor the operator, but not to intervene if the operator was not paying attention.

In court, however, the operator was found guilty of homicide; Uber was found not guilty. Less than a year later, they were operating self-driving cars again, despite employees reporting to the New York Times[^3] that they believed Uber was taking shortcuts to hit their internal milestones. They did, two years later, ultimately sell off the self-driving unit of their business, but their company seems healthy enough, with its stock at nearly twice its pre-2020 IPO value, hugely outperforming their primary competitor, Lyft, whose stock has fallen more than 50% in the same period.

This still does not directly address the question of what happens when a corporation harms the citizens of a country via their own government; but it does tell us that even when legal processes are in place which should create risk, the risk still often ends up displaced onto individual workers or onto the public at large.

---

I recently came across a sewing machine manual from the 1960s, which spent several pages explaining why they had chosen to use nylon gears, because they were clearly worried that customers would believe it was a sign they were cutting corners. Today, we just expect corners to be cut – and accept it as though we have no choice in the matter (because we so rarely do).

And even if we did care and did have a choice, wealth concentration means that our choice does not matter. Capitalism is often defended with the claim that it is a system which optimizes for what people want. This is not strictly true: Capitalism optimizes for profit, and profit is not strictly the same as what the public wants.[^4] Given the choice to build products for mega-corporations or to build products for the public, where is the profit? Surely we all recognize by now that Facebook is not a product for its users, but for its advertisers.

There is little to no risk to a company from the public, then; and when even our government fails to protect our interests against corporate interests, what other risk is there?

The answer, of course, is the risk of being overtaken or supplanted by a competitor.

---

To preserve their lead over competitors, corporations will try almost anything. This is the primary risk they face, since the other main sources of risk have been neutered. And so incentive structures are put in place to demand faster and faster delivery of products and features; mistakes whose risk lies in the domain of public or governmental reaction are immaterial.

Consider, perhaps, why European tech companies seem to lag American tech companies. It is not a lack of talent, nor of leadership. Rather, it is a regulatory environment which imposes some real risk.[^5] Posed with real risk, they must be more deliberate; they cannot sacrifice users in their entirety for the sake of maximum profits.

American tech companies are increasingly unencumbered by risks imposed by the societies they operate in, and are instead exposed only to the risk of competing with companies who are ever more exploitative.

And so we return to incompetence: It is nowhere to be found. The workers are competent, of course. But even the executives are competent; they simply do not care about protecting the public if it comes at the expense of profit. And why should they, given that there is no downside to risking public welfare? Rather than incompetent, these leaders are *irresponsible*.[^6] Not irresponsible toward themselves or even their businesses, either, but toward the public at large. Their ideas of success are not only misaligned with their workers' ideas of success, but of the public's ideas of success at large.

Perhaps instead of discussing model alignment, then, we should be discussing oligarch alignment? It seems like an easier thing to pin down, at any rate.
